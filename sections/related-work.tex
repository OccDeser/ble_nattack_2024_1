\section{Related Work} \label{sec:7related-work}

We discuss five lines of prior work: empirical studies of low-entropy vulnerabilities, enhanced
attacker models for specific primitives, protocol-specific reveal rules, equivalence-based
analysis of off-line guessing, and probabilistic model checking.

\noindent\textbf{Empirical studies of low-entropy vulnerabilities.}
Early studies~\cite{gong1993protecting,kelsey1997secure} highlighted the profound impact of low-entropy keys on cryptographic-protocol security.
In practice, a long list of severe vulnerabilities has been attributed to low-entropy cryptographic variables, including those disclosed in~\cite{jangid2023bluepe,schneier1998cryptanalysis,ferguson1999cryptographic,gpgcracking} in addition to the KNOB and PMKID attacks examined in this paper.
These studies identify the problem but do not offer an analysis methodology.

\noindent\textbf{Enhanced attacker models for specific primitives.}
Several works extend symbolic tools with enhanced attackers against specific cryptographic primitives, such as weak hash functions~\cite{cheval2023hash}, unsafe Diffie-Hellman groups~\cite{cremers2019prime}, malleable digital signatures~\cite{jackson2019sign}, and AEAD schemes~\cite{cremers2023aead}.
These works target primitive-level weaknesses rather than key-length-induced weaknesses, and typically require modifications to the tool's internal term-rewriting logic.
None of them addresses keys that are weak because of their length, or the decomposition of a
compound key into separately breakable parts.

\noindent\textbf{Protocol-specific reveal rules.}
Shi et al.~\cite{shi2023blesc} proposed an ad-hoc approach to discover brute-force-associated attacks in BLE, where a fixed reveal rule allows the attacker to compromise one particular key.
Claverie et al.~\cite{claverie2023tamarin} and Jangid et al.~\cite{jangid2023bluepe} follow a similar protocol-specific pattern to enable compromise of a single cryptographic variable in BLE pairing.
These approaches are tailored to a known attack and cannot generalize, because (i) the reveal rule hard-codes one specific function symbol, (ii) there is no notion of compound-key decomposition, and (iii) no graded-attacker model constrains which keys are actually within reach of a realistic adversary.
In contrast, our \texttt{MDerive} abstraction is function-symbol agnostic, our three-tier oracle design supports recursive decomposition of compound keys, and our SA/QA attacker model anchors breakability to a concrete bit-level bound.

\noindent\textbf{Equivalence-based analysis of off-line guessing.}
A separate line of work decides whether an attacker can tell a correct guess from a wrong one, by
asking whether two systems that differ only in the guessed value are indistinguishable.
Baudet~\cite{baudet2005guessing} gives the definition in this style, and ProVerif checks it with
diff-equivalence~\cite{blanchet2005equivalences}.
On the \Tamarin{} side, observational equivalence~\cite{basin2015obseq} provides the underlying
method, and Parad\v{z}ik and Derek~\cite{paradzik2022conditional} extend it with conditions to
obtain the first verification of off-line guessing resistance in the multiset rewriting setting,
applied to the EAP-EKE password-authenticated key exchange.
Their question is whether a guess is checkable at all, which is decided from the messages the
protocol emits, and it covers candidate validation that our attacker model leaves out
(\autoref{sec:threat-model}).
What the answer does not record is how large the guessed value is. A 128-bit key that is checkable
in principle receives the same verdict as a 4-byte one, so the verdict alone does not separate a
key that a given attacker can enumerate from one that no attacker can.
That separation is what the low-entropy annotation and the graded attacker of
\autoref{sec:threat-model} supply.
We also work in \Tamarin{}'s trace mode, so a recovered value is an ordinary term in the
attacker's knowledge and remains usable in later rules and in other sessions, which is what the
UMD attack needs.
The two lines are complementary. An equivalence check tells whether a guess can be confirmed, and
our analysis tells whether confirming it is within a stated attacker's reach.

\noindent\textbf{Probabilistic model checking.}
Siedlecka-Lamch et al.~\cite{siedlecka2016probabilistic} developed a probabilistic model checker for security protocols.
Given a per-key compromise probability, the tool computes a risk tree and outputs a sensitivity assessment.
This line of work is complementary to ours: probabilistic tools quantify leakage risk but cannot produce the kind of concrete attack trace that a symbolic verifier produces, nor can they surface protocol-level multi-step attacks such as UMD.

\noindent\textbf{Summary.}
Across the works above we are not aware of one that combines protocol-agnostic modeling,
compound-key decomposition, and a graded bit-level attacker in a trace-based verifier without
modifying it.
Equivalence-based analyses decide a different and in places broader question, as discussed above,
but do not relate a guess to an attacker's budget.
EntropyVerif occupies that gap, and used it to find a previously unreported protocol-level attack
on Bluetooth Classic.
