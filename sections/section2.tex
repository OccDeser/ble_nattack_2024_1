\section{Background} \label{sec:2background}

In this section, we provide an overview of the concepts of entropy and brute-force attacks.
Then we discuss some wide-known protocols and the low-entropy keys involved in them.
We introduce \Tamarin{}\cite{meier2013tamarin}, one of state-of-the-art symbolic tools for cryptographic protocol verification.

\subsection{Entropy and brute force attacks}

Entropy was introduced by Shannon \cite{shannon1948communicationtheory}, to quantify the randomness and uncertainty within a system.
In the domain of cryptography, the entropy of a key serves as a crucial metric for assessing its strength.
% Formally, consider a uniformly distributed key~$K$ within a key space of size~$N$.
% The entropy of such a key can be represented by \autoref{eq:entropy_definition}.
% \begin{equation} 
%     H(K) = \log_2(N) \label{eq:entropy_definition}
% \end{equation}
% where~$H(K)$ denotes the entropy of the key~$K$.
% This equation illustrates that the entropy of a uniformly distributed key is equivalent to its bit length.
% For an adversary employing exhaustive search techniques to locate the key, the number of keys that must be tested is~$2^{H(K)}$.
% Therefore, an increase in the key's entropy directly correlates with a higher level of difficulty for an attacker attempting to restore the key.

The method of trying all possible keys until the correct one is called a brute force attack.
Brute force attacks can be categorized into two types, online and offline attacks, based on whether they require real-time interaction with the target system.
In an online attack, the adversary must persistently send the attempted keys to the target system to verify their correctness.
In an offline one, the attacker does not need to interact with the target system but only needs to obtain the ciphertext and attempt all possible keys to decrypt it.
Since online attacks require continuous interaction with the target system, they are less efficient than offline attacks, which means that we need stronger keys to resist offline attacks.
In this paper, we primarily focus on the offline brute-force attacks.
Throughout the subsequent sections, any reference to brute-force attacks, unless explicitly stated otherwise, pertains to offline brute-force attacks.

\subsection{Protocols tolerating low-entropy keys}  
Low-entropy keys applied in cryptographic protocols are not rare in practice. 
Just as we mentioned before, the compatibility for old devices, the power consumption for low-power devices, and the user experience prevent protocols from guaranteeing all keys of them possess high entropy.
The use of low-entropy keys is a trade-off between security and practicality. 
Numerous widely used protocols are tolerant with low-entropy keys, such as WPA2-PSK, Bluetooth Low Energy and Bluetooth Classic.


\subsubsection{WPA2-PSK}
Wi-Fi Protected Access 2 Pre-Shared-Key~(WPA2-PSK) is a protocol used to establish a secure connection between a device~(supplicant) and an access point~(authenticator) in a personal Wi-Fi network, such as home networks and cafés.
\autoref{fig:4-way-handshake} illustrates the complete process of this protocol, 
    where the supplicant and authenticator negotiate a Pairwise Transient Key~$PTK$ from a Pairwise Master Key~$PMK$ that is derived from a pre-shared $Passphrase$ and the Wi-Fi network name~$SSID$.
The authenticator then distributes the Group Temporal Key~$GTK$, protecting broadcast messages, to the supplicant.

\begin{figure}[t]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.870780\columnwidth]{img/4-way-handshake} 
    \caption{WPA2-PSK}
    \label{fig:4-way-handshake}
\end{figure}

WPA2-PSK contains a 4-way handshake protocol. 
Before the 4-way handshake, the user configures the same $Passphrase$ on the supplicant and the authenticator.
WPA2-PSK uses the key derivation function PBKDF2 to generate $PMK$ from $Passphrase$ and $SSID$, as shown in \autoref{eq:pmk}.
% \autoref{eq:pmk} shows the calculation of $PMK$.
\begin{equation} \label{eq:pmk}
    PMK = \text{PBKDF2}(Passphrase, SSID)
\end{equation}
$Passphrase$ is a secret phrase consisting of 8 to 63 characters.
It is worth noting that if a user selects a $Passphrase$ from a limited character set, such as digits, it significantly reduces the entropy of the $PMK$, which makes WPA2-PSK vulnerable to attackers.
The PMKID attack exploits this vulnerability, and we will discuss this attack in detail in \autoref{sec:wpa2-psk}.

Based on the shared $PMK$, the authenticator and supplicant perform the 4-way handshake.
First, the authenticator and supplicant exchange random values $N_A$ and $N_B$ in message 1 and 2. 
Then they calculate $PTK$ as follows:
\begin{equation}
    \begin{split}
        PTK = \text{PRF}(PMK,{MAC}_{A},{MAC}_\text{S}, N_A, N_B)
    \end{split} 
\end{equation}
Here, $PRF$ is a pseudo-random function. 
${MAC}_{A}$ and ${MAC}_\text{S}$ denote the MAC addresses of the authenticator and supplicant, respectively.
The $PTK$ is then used to ensure the secrecy and integrity of the communication between the supplicant and authenticator.
Subsequently, the authenticator transmits the $GTK$ to the supplicant in message 3, which is used as an encryption key in multicast communications.
Finally, the supplicant sends message 4 to the authenticator to confirm the handshake's integrity.

It is important to note that, according to IEEE 802.11i~\cite{80211i}, the 4-way handshake is composed of four EAPOL-key messages.
The first EAPOL-key message carries an additional field, $PMKID$, which assists devices in rapidly completing authentication when reconnecting.
$PMKID$ is calculated as \autoref{eq:pmkid}.
\begin{equation} \label{eq:pmkid}
    \begin{split}
        PMKID = \text{HMAC}(PMK, {MAC}_{A} \| {MAC}_{S})
    \end{split} 
\end{equation}

\subsubsection{Bluetooth Low Energy}

Bluetooth Low Energy~(BLE), introduced in 2010~\cite{bluetooth4}, is a protocol used for short-range wireless communication with low power consumption.
% It was introduced in Bluetooth Core Version 4.0\cite{bluetooth4}. 
The BLE pairing protocol shares a long-term key between two BLE devices~(initiator and responder), to ensure the confidentiality and integrity of subsequent data transmissions.
\autoref{fig:ble-pairing} shows the BLE pairing process, where the two devices negotiate a long-term key~$LTK$, which is used to derive a session key~$SK$ to establish an encrypted session for transmitting specific keys. 
% They then use $LTK$ to derive a session key~$SK$ to establish an encrypted session for transmitting specific keys.

\begin{figure}[t]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.912689\columnwidth]{img/ble-pairing} 
    \caption{BLE pairing}
    \label{fig:ble-pairing}
\end{figure}

% In the BLE pairing protocol, the device initiating the pairing is referred to as the Initiator, while the device responding to the pairing is referred to as the Responder.
At the beginning of the BLE pairing process, both devices exchange their pairing features, including the IO capabilities and the key lengths.
% ! Rewrite following sentence.
They select the shorter of the two key lengths as the valid data length $KeySize$ for the subsequently negotiated $LTK$.
During the authentication phase, both devices select an appropriate pairing method based on their IO capabilities.
After this phase, they successfully negotiate an intermediate key $LTK'$.
The $LTK'$ is truncated based on the previously negotiated $KeySize$, forming the $LTK$.
% For brevity, we consider only the truncated portion of the $LTK$, ignoring the zero-padding portion.
For example, if $KeySize$ is 16, $LTK'[1:16]$ will be the $LTK$, where $LTK'[1:16]$ denotes the first 16 bytes of $LTK'$.
After the LTK is generated, the initiator and the responder exchange four random values, $IV_I$, $IV_R$, $SKD_I$ and $SKD_R$.
These values, along with the LTK, are used to calculate the session key~$SK$, as shown in \autoref{eq:sk}.
\begin{equation} \label{eq:sk}
    SK = \text{AES-CCM}_{LTK}(IV_I\|IV_R, SKD_I\|SKD_R)
\end{equation}
Then, the two devices use the $SK$ to encrypt the session, safeguarding subsequent communication data.

\subsubsection{Bluetooth Classic}
Bluetooth Classic~(BC), also referred to as Basic Rate/Enhanced Date Rate~(BR/EDR), is the initial Bluetooth standard, 
predominantly designed for applications requiring high data transfer rates such as audio streaming, file transfer and serial communication.
\autoref{fig:bc-session} illustrates the session establishment protocol between paired BC devices.
Similarly, BC devices use the BC pairing protocol to negotiate a long-term link key~$LK$ between the central device and the peripheral device.
After the pairing procedure, the session establishment protocol use the $LK$ to derive a session key~$SK$ to ensure the session security.
After the pairing procedure, the session establishment protocol uses the $LK$ to perform authentication and generate an Authenticated Channel Offset~($ACO$). 
The central and peripheral devices then use the $ACO$ and $LK$ to derive the session key~($SK$).

\begin{figure}[t]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.902212\columnwidth]{img/bc-session} 
    \caption{BC Session}
    \label{fig:bc-session}
\end{figure}

% Prior to the establishment of the session, both devices share~$LK$.
In the very beginning of the session establishment procedure, the central device and peripheral build a link connection first. 
In this phase, the devices exchange features supported by them, like $FeatSC$ which means whether the device supports \emph{Secure Connections}.
After the link connection is established, the two devices start the authentication phase. 
There are two methods to authenticate the devices:
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=*]
    \item \textbf{Legacy Authentication}: Only the central device will authenticate the peripheral device.
    \item \textbf{Secure Authentication}: Both devices will authenticate each other.
\end{itemize}
In the both authentication methods, the two devices would use $LK$ to compute the Authenticated Channel Offset~$ACO$ based on the random value(s).
If both devices' $FeatSC$ is \emph{True}, they will use the secure authentication method, otherwise, they will use the legacy one. 
In the Legacy Authentication, only the central device will authenticate the peripheral device.
The central device will send a random value $AuRand$ to the peripheral device to authenticate the peripheral device.
In the Secure Authentication, a mutual authentication will be performed.
Both the central and peripheral devices will exchange two random values $AuR_C$ and $AuR_P$ to authenticate each other.
\autoref{eq:legacy-aco} and \autoref{eq:secure-aco} illustrate how $ACO$ is calculated in Legacy and Secure Authentication, respectively.
\begin{equation} \label{eq:legacy-aco}
    SERS, ACO = \text{E1}(LK, AuRand)
\end{equation}
\begin{equation} \label{eq:secure-aco}
    SERS_C, SERS_P, ACO = \text{h5}(LK, AuR_C\|AuR_P)
\end{equation}
In the following phase, the central and peripheral devices engage in the negotiation of the session key's length.
In the final phase, the central device sends a random value~$EnRand$, to the peripheral device.
The two devices then utilize the $LK$, $EnRand$ and $ACO$ to derive the session key~$SK'$.
But in \emph{Secure Connections}, $EnRand$ will be ignored and the $SK'$ will be derived from $LK$ and $ACO$.
\autoref{eq:legacy-sk} and \autoref{eq:secure-sk} illustrate how $SK'$ is calculated in Legacy and Secure Connections, respectively.
\begin{equation} \label{eq:legacy-sk}
    SK' = \text{E3}(LK, EnRand, ACO)
\end{equation}
\begin{equation} \label{eq:secure-sk}
    SK' = \text{h3}(LK, ACO)
\end{equation}
After that, the central device and peripheral device will take a few bytes of $SK'$ as the session key~$SK$ based on the negotiated key length.
This truncating operation is the same as the one in BLE pairing protocol.
The peripheral device then encrypts an \Tfunction{ACCEPT} message using $SK$ and sends it to the central device.
The successful decryption of this message by the central device signifies the establishment of the encrypted session.

\subsection{\Tamarin{}}
The \Tamarin{} prover provides a powerful and expressive language for modeling protocols and properties, allowing researchers to analyze the security of their designs rigorously.
\Tamarin{} supports a wide range of cryptographic primitives and protocols, making it a versatile tool for formal analysis.
In \Tamarin{}, we use terms to represent messages and the behaviors of protocols and adversaries are captured through multiset rewriting rules. 
\Tamarin{} allows using logic formulas to specific security properties of protocols as well.

\subsubsection{Term algebra}
Messages in symbolic models are typically represented as terms in a term algebra. 
A term is an expression built from symbols that encode the data exchanged in the protocol. 
The symbolic model abstracts away the concrete cryptographic operations, representing them through algebraic terms that capture their logical structure.
For example, the encryption of a message~$m$ with a key~$k$ can be represented as a term:~$\text{senc}(m, k)$. 
Here,~$\text{senc}$ is a function symbol representing symmetric encryption operation.
Formally, we assume a signature~$\Sigma$ defining the set of such function or operator symbols and their arity and a countably infinite set of variables~$\mathcal{V}$. 
Similarly, a countable set~$\mathcal{N}$ is defined as the set of names (like keys, nonces, public identifiers etc.). 
The set of terms is the closure of~$\mathcal{V}$ and~$\mathcal{N}$ under the operators in~$\Sigma$ and donated as~$T_\Sigma(\mathcal{V}, \mathcal{N})$.
A \emph{message} is a special \emph{term} which doesn't contain any variables.
We use equations to specify the algebraic properties of cryptographic functions.
An equation is a pair of terms, denoted as~$t_1 = t_2$, which specifies that the~$t_1$ can be substituted by~$t_2$, for~$t_1,t_2 \in T_\Sigma(\mathcal{V})$.
For instance, the equation~$\text{sdec}(\text{senc}(m, k), k) = m$ models the property that the decryption of an encryption with the same key yields the original message.

\subsubsection{Multiset rewriting rules}
Multiset rewriting system are used to model protocols and adversaries in \Tamarin{}. 
Every state of the transition system is a multiset of facts. 
A \emph{fact} is defined as~$F(t_1, \ldots, t_n)$, for~$t_i \in T_\Sigma(\mathcal{V}, \mathcal{N})$.
The transitions of states are defined by labeled multiset rewriting rules, which is denoted as~$F_l \xrightarrow{F_a} F_r$.
Here,~$F_l$,~$F_a$ and~$F_r$ are the \emph{left-hand side}, \emph{action} and \emph{right-hand side} facts of the rule, respectively.
When all of the facts in~$F_l$ are present in the current state, the rule can be applied, and the facts in~$F_a$ are consumed, while the facts in~$F_r$ are added to the new state.
There are two sorts of facts: linear facts and persistent facts.
Linear facts can only be consumed once after their creation, while persistent facts can be consumed an unlimited number of times.
Action facts in~$F_a$ represent the label of the rule and can be used to restrict executions of the rule or to verify properties of the protocol.

\subsubsection{Logic formulas}
Trace properties are usually expressed as first-order logic formulas which are evaluated over the traces (states sequences) of the protocol.
The trace properties in \Tamarin{} are defined as first-order logic formulas with time points using action facts.
When it comes to cryptographic protocols, secrecy or authentication are usually included in the trace properties to be verified.
For example, the secrecy requires that an installed key~$k$ cannot be known by adversaries in any trace of the protocol.
This property can be encoded in the following lemma:
\begin{flalign*}
    & \hspace{0em} \TAll \;\, \text{\Tterm{k \#i.}} \;\, \text{\TFact{Install(k)@\#i}} &\\
    & \hspace{3em} \implies \TNot (\TEx \;\, \text{\TLemma{\#j.}} \;\, \text{\TFact{K(k)@\#j}} ) &
\end{flalign*}
This formula describes that for all time points~$i$, if a key~$k$ is installed at time~$i$, then there doesn't exist a time point~$j$ at which the key~$k$ is known by an adversary.
